How Could There Be Life When There Is Death? Lessons from Grief

This blog is about a dream that keeps visiting me, the people I've lost, and a conversation with a grieving mother that I can't stop thinking about. I don't have the answers, but I'd like to share what I've learned so far. Writing about it is my way of making sense of grief.

The dream

On a random day, I came across a young kid who kept asking an old man, "Pwede ta mo laag menteryo?" It sounded strange. I wondered why a child would choose the cemetery over the plaza.

Then I saw them walking together, each holding a stick broom and a small brush, heading into the cemetery. At the entrance, they stopped at a tomb, and I assumed a family member was laid to rest there. From the corner of my eye, I saw them light candles, then sweep and brush the tomb. The kid was so happy and innocent, as if the place were her playground.

I left. After that, the kid and the old man began visiting me in my dreams.

The day my lolo fell asleep

On the morning of Wednesday, January 15, my lolo was snoring so loudly that everyone in the house could hear it. My lola thought he was sleeping peacefully. "Haystt, maayo ra sad kay nakatulog na," she thought. But morning became lunch, lunch became dinner, and he was still asleep.

I was at school when Mama contacted me to go to my grandparents' house. After class, I headed to the gate, where Mama picked me up in a white van.

When we arrived, all the apo were welcomed by the sound of his heavy snoring. While we waited for him to wake up, we sang along to his Kenny Rogers songs playing on the speaker at the altar table.

At 8:45 PM, his snoring grew softer and lighter. Then he peacefully stopped breathing. He had been diagnosed with a stroke, and what we thought was deep sleep was actually a coma.

What came before

In December 2024, my Tito Omar, the husband of my mama's sister, passed away from diabetes. He was close to my lolo, who had been bedridden since September 2024. When Lolo heard the news, he couldn't see Tito Omar one last time, not even until the day he was laid to rest.

I can't help thinking that not being able to see someone so important to him made Lolo's condition worse. After Tito Omar's death, Lolo could no longer speak. Then came the day he fell into a coma.

After they were gone

Sundays became ordinary days. There were no more family gatherings and no more time together. It stayed that way for months, until slowly, the family began finding meaning in Sundays again.

Grief still visits me on some days, and I dream of the loved ones who have passed. The kid and the old man were me and my lolo, remembered through my memories of him. The dream returns most often on the days when grief weighs on me the most.
